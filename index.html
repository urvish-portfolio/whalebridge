Where the operator in me started

At university, the clue came from an event.

It was a non-technical event at an engineering college, running on a fixed budget in a place where the technical events were supposed to draw the crowd. We negotiated what we could, visited nearby colleges to market it ourselves, and drew more participants than any other event. On the day, the problems arrived one after another. Solving them, keeping people moving and watching the whole thing come together was what made operations click for me.

At Vikram Engineering Works the setting was a factory floor: 33 people, zero accidents, and customers in 11 states at the other end of the supply chain. The lesson I kept from there is that solving most of a problem still counts. Nobody expects a problem to vanish completely before the work is worth doing.

Then came Birmingham. When I joined Greggs, the store had no manager, training had stopped and daily sales had fallen by 25–30%. On my second day a supervisor quit, leaving me on the front counter before I had been trained on the till or the products. That night I nearly called my manager to quit. Instead the next shift became another problem to solve, and I ran that store without a manager above me while covering two other sites every week.

The fix came from what the system was asking the team to do. Production was reshaped around the real sales pattern, savouries were baked closer to demand, ordering followed weekdays, weather and local events, and a third person joined the peak so the sandwich maker could stay on sandwiches. Sales grew by 20% and waste fell by 30% with no extra resources.

Fresh Bake was a brand opening its very first store, in Leicester. We built everything a new team needs from nothing, and from 3 June 2024 I ran the store on my own. That story is case 3.

At Shake Shack I trained at Canary Wharf, then opened Birmingham as one of a four-person management team, with two assistant managers reporting to me at opening and three later. Shake Shack grew out of Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group in New York, and its service standards come from fine dining. I owned the site's numbers week by week: sales against budget, labour, cost of goods and waste. In my last week, a full independent audit scored the site 99.9%.

The next step is to run a venue of my own, where the standard of every service is mine to set. A harbourside restaurant that guests choose for a special occasion is where that standard matters most.

Case 1 · Opening a site

Fifteen days of queues

Shake Shack Birmingham opened in December 2024, and the queue came back every day for 15 days.

Most of the 35 to 40 people on each shift were brand new. Deliveries were huge, stakeholders were on site, and a food-safety inspector could walk in unannounced at any time, so compliance had to be ready alongside the guest experience. It was the biggest challenge of my hospitality career.

The work started weeks earlier, with suppliers, the management roster and the leave plan. Through the opening weeks I forecast each day's sales, set the forecast against budget and wrote the floorplan to match: every person placed by station, every break covered.

One thing I would change. At Birmingham, part of the team came through other people's hiring. For the next opening I want to hire the whole team myself, so that everyone on the first shift knows me and I have approved every hire.

How it wentMore than £80,000 a week and over 1,000 guests a day through the opening weeks. The site went on to a 5-star food hygiene rating, 99% in health and safety and 92%+ in brand audits.

Case 2 · A shift under pressure

11:00, the doors open

At 10:30 on 28 May 2025, the queue was already forming behind the shutter.

International Burger Day meant buy-one-get-one-free on every burger. Before the doors opened, the shape of the day was already visible.

Then, at 11:00, the back door brought another problem: a delivery of about £4,000 of fresh and frozen stock. Everyone had over-ordered for what was expected to be a record day, and the delivery could not sit outside.

Two slower team members came off Expo to put it away. That left me alone on Expo with 15–20 orders on the screen while the queue kept moving.

Most of the team knew most stations. When their own station came under control, people came over to help. The operation could move people towards the pressure point instead of leaving everyone fixed to their original position.

At 11:30 the dining room needed help, so I moved there. Once service was back on time, I stepped off the stations and watched the whole operation: restocking, compliance, temperature checks and guests. After 15:00, breaks were covered two at a time. The day finished at about £11,000 after discounts, and the team enjoyed the shift as much as I did.

What I took from itCross-training turns a staffing plan into a pool of options. The manager's job is to keep moving between the immediate problem and the whole floor.

Case 3 · A brand's first store

Building it, then running it

Fresh Bake was a new brand, and Leicester was its first store. Before the doors opened, I co-designed the layout with the Operations Director, worked on the sales scenarios, worked through the snagging list and hired the opening team.

I was the youngest person at the planning table, and many of my ideas were tested against other suggestions first. More often than not, they were the ones the store kept. I learned to make the case with results rather than rank.

On 3 June 2024 my manager left. From then until I moved on in August, I ran the store on my own. My contract said 45 hours a week; most weeks I worked 65 to 70, because I wanted the store to succeed.

After hours I researched local suppliers and negotiated until the numbers moved: uniforms about 40% cheaper and ingredients about 5% cheaper. The Operations Director checked the uniform deal himself before we switched.

If you work as an owner, the store will be the best.

Case 4 · People

The hire I nearly didn't make

He was new to the UK, still finding his words, and the interview did not go smoothly. My first instinct at Fresh Bake was to say no.

Then I remembered my own second day at Greggs: alone on a counter, unable to follow half of what customers said, and close to quitting that night. Someone had given me the chance to get through that week. So I hired him, and trained him on guest experience until the counter felt like his.

After I left Fresh Bake, he became the store's Assistant Manager. It is one of the things I am proudest of.

At Shake Shack, a new team member was so slow that I was close to ending his probation. After a shift I talked to him as a friend rather than a manager, and what he told me changed how I managed him. He showed me how to delegate to him, and two months later he was Employee of the Month. When I left, every member of the team wrote in my farewell card.

What I took from itI hire for energy and attitude, because everything else can be taught. And when someone is struggling, the conversation comes before the decision.

How I work

Six habits, each learned on a real shift. Hover or focus a card to open it.

Every corner is mine

Open

Every corner is mine

I want to be proud of what I am building. There is no “that’s the owner’s problem” and no “that’s not in my job description”: the whole restaurant is mine, so every problem in it is mine to fix. What counts is the result, not the hours it takes.

Go to the table yourself

Open

Go to the table yourself

When something goes wrong, the manager goes to the guest: acknowledge it, apologise, put it right with something extra. Then the cause gets logged and fixed, so the next guest never meets it.

Tell the team why

Open

Tell the team why

Every shift starts with a briefing: the day's forecast, what we are focusing on, and why we are asking for it. When people know the purpose of the day, they feel part of the team rather than just staff, and accountability follows.

Walk the travel path

Open

Walk the travel path

Every 1.5 to 2 hours, a full walk from the door to the stores: host stand, dining room, stations, temperatures, stock. A shortage or a compliance slip shows up before a guest or an inspector finds it.

Remember the regulars

Open

Remember the regulars

Names, usual orders, the table they like. Small details, written down and shared with the team, turn a second visit into a habit.

Know people personally

Open

Know people personally

I talk to people as a friend first. People confide more when the relationship goes beyond instructions, and that reveals problems a report will never show.

Why Whalebridge?

Sunday Co. was built by operators who ran some of Sydney's best-known venues, and it talks about teams that feel confident, proud and inspired to deliver world-class hospitality. That is the culture I trained in at Shake Shack, where hospitality standards come from Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group.

Whalebridge has a view guests will cross the city for. The work is making sure the service is the reason they return, and that is the measure I have been judged on.

What will you bring with you?

Skills I have used week in, week out, each one backed by the rotas, trackers, reports and guides I worked from.

Opening restaurants
Two openings. For Fresh Bake's first store: the layout co-designed with the Operations Director, pre-launch sales scenarios, the snagging list worked through, the opening team hired, and the learning plan, checklists and guides built with the team. For Shake Shack Birmingham: suppliers, the management roster and leave plan set up before the doors opened, training days on full floorplans, trial runs before busy periods, and food for the neighbours to build goodwill.
Forecasting and planning
Daily and weekly sales forecasts I set myself, with labour, prep and each station's deployment planned to them, and POS sales and labour data checked every day.
Guest experience
Guest feedback read every day and shared in the briefing, low scorers contacted, complaints handled in person and fixed at the cause, regulars remembered, table touches and food running in the dining room. Likelihood to return raised from 8.1 to 9.3.
Running the numbers
The site P&L reviewed every period and acted on: labour, cost of goods and controllable costs. A weekly commercial report covering sales against budget, labour, average spend and sales per labour hour. A cost-of-goods report twice a month, with stock variances traced line by line. Crew labour held under 26% of sales against a 30% target.
Stock and suppliers
Weekly orders to 11 suppliers across about 180 product lines, placed to each supplier's cut-off. Every delivery checked against its purchase order, with shortages and credits chased. Local suppliers researched and negotiated with for a new store: uniforms about 40% cheaper and ingredients about 5% cheaper.
Food safety and standards
A 5-star food hygiene rating, 99.9% in a full independent audit, 98.71% in the NSF food safety audit, 99% in health and safety and 92%+ in brand audits. The travel path walked every 1.5 to 2 hours. Food Safety, Fire Marshal and First Aid at Level 2.
Building teams
Hiring for energy and attitude, because everything else can be trained. Crew learning plans and assessments, cross-training across stations, a daily pre-shift briefing, and coaching every task against a time limit. Presenting and influencing
The weekly numbers walked through with my area manager, the site presented on brand-audit walk-rounds to senior stakeholders from the US, and ideas that won through at Fresh Bake once the results were on the table.
Process and data
An engineer's eye for bottlenecks from a B.Tech and three and a half years in manufacturing, 5S and planograms on the shop floor, an MBA in Operations Management, BCS business-analysis certificates, and Excel and Power BI for sales forecasting.
